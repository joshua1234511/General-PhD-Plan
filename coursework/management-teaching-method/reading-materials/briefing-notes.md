# Management Teaching Methods — briefing notes

Section-wise abstract notes + questions to ask, so you can skim and be class-ready.
Covers the two readings and the in-class exercise.

---

## Reading 1 — *Ready To Be Counted: The Research Case for Education Policy Action on Non-Cognitive Skills*

Gabrieli, Ansel & Krachman (2015), Transforming Education working paper, v1.0 Dec 2015

**One-line abstract.** A synthesis of economics/psychology/education research
(several thousand children) arguing that **non-cognitive skills** — not just
cognitive ability — drive academic success, career outcomes, and lifelong
well-being, and therefore deserve deliberate policy and classroom attention.

**Section-wise notes**

- **The distinction.** Two skill sets matter: *cognitive* (knowledge, reasoning)
  and *non-cognitive*. Non-cognitive splits into:
  - **Intrapersonal** (self-directed): interest, curiosity, persistence,
    self-control, self-regulation.
  - **Interpersonal** (other-directed): **empathy, listening, cooperation**.
- **Academics.** Non-cognitive skills predict high-school & college completion;
  correlate with higher K-12 and college achievement; fostering them as early as
  pre-school has both immediate and long-term impact.
- **Career.** Employers value and seek them; they predict likelihood of employment;
  stronger childhood skills predict higher adult earnings and financial stability.
- **Well-being.** Linked to less crime/incarceration, fewer unplanned teen births,
  and better health (lower mortality, obesity, smoking, substance abuse, mental-health issues).

**Questions to ask in class**

- If non-cognitive skills are this predictive, *can they actually be taught* — or
  are we just selecting for students who already have them? What's the evidence on
  intervention vs. selection?
- How do you **assess** empathy/persistence in a management classroom without
  reducing them to a tick-box rubric (the measurement problem)?
- Where's the line between *developing* interpersonal skills and *manipulating*
  disposition? (ethics of "teaching character")

## Reading 2 — *Great Teachers Don't Teach*

Ben Johnson (2013), Edutopia blog

**One-line abstract.** Provocation: the best teachers stop "telling" and instead
*engineer learning experiences* that put students in the driver's seat — learning
by doing, not by listening. Grounded in Dewey's experiential learning /
constructivism.

**Section-wise notes**

- **The hook.** The author's most memorable professor is remembered for the
  *experiments students did*, not the lectures — experience creates long-term memory.
- **Good vs. great.** Good teachers care, know content, explain well, perform/tell
  stories, demand high performance. **Great** teachers go further: they "maneuver
  students into the driver's seat, then get out of the way."
- **In the classroom.** Three named methods:
  - **Project-based learning** — give an urgent reason to learn, then let students
    show mastery by doing.
  - **Inquiry** — keep students curious to discover.
  - **Lecture** is the anti-pattern: "teachers talking… while students daydream."
- **Deeper methods.** **Socratic method** (answer questions with questions);
  **performance-based learning** ("deep end of the pool" — the swimming lesson
  becomes relevant only when you need it).
- **Taking action.** Great teachers "stack the deck" so students can't help but
  learn — mostly by teaching themselves; knowledge becomes permanent, not illusory.
  Reframe the role: **"learning engineer," not "teacher."**

**Questions to ask in class**

- Is "don't teach" a false binary? When is **direct instruction** genuinely
  superior (novices, safety-critical skills, foundational scaffolding)?
- Does student-driven learning **scale** to a 60–80 person MBA/PhD cohort, or only
  small classes? What breaks?
- How do you reconcile "get out of the way" with the course's own **facilitation**
  and **feedback** emphasis — isn't skilled facilitation still heavy teacher work,
  just invisible?
- For a **practice-teaching module:** which non-lecture method fits your topic best
  — case, simulation (like Win-As-Much-As-You-Can), or Socratic?

## In-class exercise — *Win As Much As You Can* (debrief prep)

The cooperation-vs-competition game (full payoff table in the
[course README](../README.md)).

**What it teaches**

- A social-dilemma / prisoner's-dilemma-style game: individually "rational"
  competitive play (always X) is **collectively destructive** — everyone loses.
- **Trust and communication** (the cluster-confer rounds 5/8/10, with ×3/×5/×10
  stakes) are where value is created or destroyed.
- Mirrors the **experiential cycle**:
  *Planning → Experience → Outcome → Reflection.*

**Questions to ask / reflect on**

- What made the cluster default to 4X (mutual defection) instead of cooperating?
  Was it the scoring, the "Win as much as *you* can" framing, or lack of trust?
- Does the "×10" final round reward betrayal so heavily that it *guarantees*
  broken trust — is the game rigged toward the lesson?
- As a **facilitator**, how would you debrief this without lecturing the moral —
  i.e. let the group *discover* the cooperation insight themselves? (this is the
  "Great Teachers Don't Teach" principle in action)

## Feedback module — readings for the feedback class

> These three readings back the graded **Feedback Project (20%)** — read the briefing
> + "Feedback Is a Two-Way Street" before the feedback class and come ready to discuss.

### Reading 3 — *Feedback Is a Two-Way Street*

Lawrence & Wiswell (1995), *Training & Development*, July: 49–52

**One-line abstract.** Constructive feedback is **two-way**, not a one-way broadcast:
it must be engaging, responsive and directed at a desired outcome, with *both* sender
and receiver sharing responsibility.

**Three elements of effective two-way feedback**

- **Specificity** — descriptive, refers to actual behaviour; the *less* specific the
  feedback, the *weaker* its impact. "You did a good job" is too vague to act on.
- **Empathy** — adds trust; seeing the other's frame of reference. **Listening is at
  the centre of empathy.** Empathy ≠ sympathy (sympathy can be paralysing; empathy is
  constructive: "I understand what you're saying… how can I help?").
- **Inquiry** — closest to the heart of two-way feedback: open-ended questions
  ("What do you think of my comments? Were they easy to follow?") that test whether
  the message was understood and invite the receiver to respond.

**Other factors:** credibility (respected, trustworthy source), timeliness (soon after
the event), and **how** it's delivered matters as much as **what** is said. Use
open-ended **inquiry instead of "why"** questions (which trigger defensiveness).
**Barriers:** power imbalance (supervisor/subordinate), competition, low trust.
*(A Feedback Self-Assessment — 20 items — is included; score 75 = satisfactory, 85 =
good, 95+ = outstanding.)*

**Questions to ask**

- If feedback should be "two-way," how does the *receiver* take responsibility without
  it becoming an argument? Where's the line between inquiry and deflection?
- Is the specificity/empathy/inquiry trio enough across a power gap (boss→subordinate)?

### Reading 4 — *Seven Keys to Effective Feedback*

Grant Wiggins (2012), *Educational Leadership*, 70(1): 10–16

**One-line abstract.** Most "feedback" is really **advice, praise or evaluation** —
none of which are feedback. True feedback is **information about how you're doing
relative to a goal**, and it works best when it has seven traits.

**The seven keys.** Effective feedback is: **goal-referenced · tangible & transparent
· actionable · user-friendly** (specific, not overwhelming) **· timely · ongoing ·
consistent.**

**Key distinctions (the sharp bit)**

- **Feedback ≠ advice** — "add more examples" is advice; give descriptive feedback
  *first*, then let the learner self-advise ("Given the feedback, how might you
  improve?").
- **Feedback ≠ evaluation/grades** — "Good job!" / "B+" carry no actionable
  information. Tip: add a mental colon — "Good work**:** your word choice was more
  precise than last time, I saw the scene clearly."
- **Less teaching + more feedback = more learning** (Hattie; Mazur's peer instruction).

**Questions to ask**

- Wiggins says feedback must be *non-judgmental* description — but doesn't a teacher
  eventually have to evaluate/grade? How do the two coexist?
- "Less teaching, more feedback" — same claim as *Great Teachers Don't Teach*. Does it
  scale to a large cohort, and what's the facilitator's cost?
- For a **practice-teaching module:** how do you build **tangible, ongoing** feedback
  *into the activity itself* (not just comments at the end)?

### Handout — *Some Aspects to Keep in Mind* (giving & receiving feedback)

From Ludlow & Panton (1996), *The Essence of Effective Communication*

**Giving:** comment on **specific actions**; give reasons; focus on **changeable
behaviour**, not the person; be **descriptive not evaluative**; opinions as opinions;
constructive not destructive; avoid loaded terms; praise in public/criticise in
private, seek solutions jointly.
**Receiving:** listen carefully; don't get defensive; **paraphrase** what you hear;
check your perception; ask for clarification/examples; don't over-react; evaluate its
accuracy and value.

> Punchline: **"Active listening and feedback go hand in hand"** — the direct bridge
> to the session on active listening.

## How to use this in the course

- **Storytelling (20%)** and **practice teaching (30%)** are the big graded pieces.
  The two readings hand you the theory (experiential/constructivist) to justify a
  **non-lecture** practice-teaching module.
- **Critical reflection (15%)** — the exercise + readings are perfect raw material;
  reflect through the *Planning→Experience→Outcome→Reflection* cycle.
- **Angle to consider:** teach your half-hour module on something in your wheelhouse
  using a **simulation or Socratic** method — practising the pedagogy while staying on
  familiar ground.
