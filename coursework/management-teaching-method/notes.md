## Session — Facilitation: facilitator vs. instructor; listening & empathy

**Input session** (Prof. Rajeev Sharma) on the course's facilitation strand —
*"theoretical & conceptual foundations of facilitation for participative learning."*
Ties directly to Objective 2 (challenges of becoming a skilled **facilitator**) and
course competency 1 (**listening deeply**).

### Facilitator vs. instructor — the core distinction

| | **Instructor** | **Facilitator** |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Transmits content; "sage on the stage" | Enables learning; "guide on the side" |
| Direction of knowledge | Teacher → students (one-way) | Drawn out *from* the group (multi-way) |
| Authority | Expert who has the answers | Process-holder; the group holds the content |
| Talk time | High (lecturing/telling) | Low — asks, listens, summarises |
| Focus | *What* is taught (coverage) | *How* people learn (experience & discovery) |
| Success looks like | Students received the material | Group reached insight largely by itself |

> Connects straight to **"Great Teachers Don't Teach"** (Johnson 2013): the
> facilitator "maneuvers students into the driver's seat, then gets out of the way."
> A facilitator is a **"learning engineer"**, not a teller.

### What's important for a facilitator (qualities emphasised)

- **Listening deeply** — the #1 competency of this course; hear content *and* feeling,
  not just wait to talk.
- **Empathy** — read the room; sense where participants are emotionally/cognitively.
- **Neutrality on content, authority on process** — hold the structure, not the "right
  answer."
- **Questioning over telling** — draw insight out (Socratic); resist the urge to
  supply the moral.
- **Managing group dynamics** — surface quiet voices, manage dominant ones, keep
  psychological safety.
- **Comfort with silence** — give thinking space; don't rush to fill gaps.
- **Reflecting back / paraphrasing** — mirror what's said so the group hears itself.

### Listening & empathy (the heart of this session)

- **Active listening is the key facilitator skill.** It is *active*, not passive: the
  facilitator listens to **understand, not to reply.** Concretely it means:
  - **Full attention** — presence, eye contact, no rehearsing your response.
  - **Paraphrasing / reflecting back** — "so what I'm hearing is…" so the group hears
    itself.
  - **Checking understanding** — clarifying questions before moving on.
  - **Withholding judgement** — don't evaluate or correct while listening.
  - **Reading non-verbals** — tone, hesitation, body language, not just words.
  - **Comfort with silence** — let a pause do the work instead of filling it.
  > Passive listening ("waiting to talk") is the anti-pattern — it looks like
  > listening but the group can tell the difference, and it kills participation.
- **Empathy** = perceiving the participant's frame of reference and signalling that
  it's understood — the interpersonal counterpart to active listening.
- Both are **interpersonal non-cognitive skills** in the Gabrieli et al. (2015)
  reading (empathy · listening · cooperation) — this session is the *pedagogical
  practice* of those very skills.
- **Facilitator move:** to debrief an exercise (e.g. *Win As Much As You Can*), listen
  and question so the group *discovers* the cooperation insight itself — rather than
  lecturing the lesson.

### Session discussion — the listener articulates what the speaker can't

**Scenario.** Someone under stress (lost a job, failed an exam) tries to explain the
cause but **can't fully put it into words**. You're listening. *Is it possible for the
listener to articulate it for them — and is that active or passive listening?*

- **Yes — and it's a hallmark of _active_ listening.** Passive listening only
  *receives*; the moment you help put the speaker's meaning into words you are
  actively engaging. The skill has names: **reflecting / paraphrasing**, or in
  Carl Rogers' client-centred terms **"reflection of feeling"** and **"accurate
  empathy."**
- **Empathy is what makes the reflection accurate**; active listening is the channel
  that delivers it. The two interpersonal skills work together here.
- **Critical caveat — do it _tentatively_ and _check_.** Offer it as a hypothesis the
  speaker can confirm or correct: *"It sounds like the failure felt like your fault,
  even though the exam was unfair — am I hearing that right?"* You are helping them
  find **their** words, not replacing them with yours.
- **Failure mode:** if you assert your interpretation as fact, it becomes
  **projection / putting words in their mouth.** Worse under stress — an emotional
  speaker may just agree ("yes, that's it") out of relief or compliance, which
  **distorts their real meaning** (false confirmation). Skilled reflection guards
  against this by inviting correction.

**Questions to ask on this:**
- Where is the line between *helpful reflection* and *projecting my own
  interpretation* onto the speaker?
- In an emotionally charged state, doesn't the speaker's tendency to just agree make
  reflection risky — how do you guard against false confirmation?
- Is "reflecting back" usable in a **classroom debrief** (group), or only in 1:1
  conversations?

### Assignment announced — practice teaching (30%)

Each participant has to **take a class to demonstrate a teaching method**:
- **Choose your own topic.**
- **Use your own methodology** (the non-lecture method you pick).
- **30 minutes each.**
> Each teaches a half-hour module using a *non-lecture* method, then gets feedback.
> A strong option: run it as a **facilitator** (this session's theme) — a simulation
> or Socratic discussion, then facilitate the debrief (active listening → questioning
> → let them conclude). → connect the topic to your own research interest.

**Questions to ask on the assignment:**
- Can the topic be **outside management**, or must it be a management topic?
- Do we submit a **lesson plan / method rationale beforehand**, or just teach on the day?
- What's the **evaluation rubric** — is it graded on the *method's execution*, the
  *learning outcome*, or the *novelty* of the method?
- Does the 30 min include **setup + debrief**, or is that 30 min of pure delivery?
- Can the method be a **simulation** (like Win-As-Much-As-You-Can) given time limits?

### Next topic — giving & receiving feedback

Session moving into **context-giving and giving/receiving feedback**; an exercise is
to come. Links to the *Role of feedback in learning and pedagogy* input session and
the graded **Project on feedback (20%)**. Giving feedback well *requires* **active
listening + empathy** (understand before you respond); receiving it well requires
listening without defensiveness.

**Questions to ask on feedback:**
- What separates **useful feedback** from praise or criticism — is there a model the
  course uses (e.g., **SBI: Situation–Behaviour–Impact**, or "feedforward")?
- How do you give feedback that keeps the learner **in the driver's seat** (the
  "Great Teachers Don't Teach" principle) rather than just telling them what to fix?
- How do you **receive** feedback without defensiveness — and is that a teachable skill?
- In a classroom debrief, how do you get *peers* to give each other good feedback?

**Feedback exercise = the graded Feedback Project (20%).** Conducted **outside class**,
in **two conditions with two _different_ partners** (people outside the MTM class,
ideally not in a boss/subordinate relation):
- **Condition A — _receiving_ negative feedback:** ask a partner to give you negative
  feedback about some aspect of yourself (~15–20 min, private, confidential).
- **Condition B — _giving_ negative feedback:** give a different partner negative
  feedback about some aspect of them (~15 min, private, confidential).
- **Brief your partner** on the nature/process of feedback from the readings first.
- **Write a report** per condition on **9 reflection dimensions** — specific vs opinion?
  constructive? descriptive vs evaluative? within their control? reaction/difficulty?
  did you use **active listening / open questions** to help them elaborate? implications
  for an academic situation? Report the *process*, **not the content**; anonymise the
  partner (gender, relationship, age-group only).
- **Evaluation:** written report + brief in-class discussion. Must **integrate the
  readings** (*Feedback Is a Two-Way Street*, *Seven Keys to Effective Feedback*, the
  *Aspects to Keep in Mind* handout) and learning from prior modules; **bring a copy to
  the designated class**.

**Facilitation question worth raising:** where does facilitation *fail* novices — for
foundational or safety-critical content, isn't some direct instruction still necessary?
How does a skilled facilitator blend the two without defaulting to telling?
