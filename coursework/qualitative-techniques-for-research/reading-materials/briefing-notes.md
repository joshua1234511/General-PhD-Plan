## Module I — Qualitative Research Design in Mgmt & Org Studies

### S1–2 · Gioia & Pitre (1990) — Multiparadigm Perspectives on Theory Building
→ full notes in [S1-prereads-prep.md](S1-prereads-prep.md). One line: theory-building
must fit paradigm (interpretivist/radical-humanist/radical-structuralist/functionalist);
bridge via transition zones + metaparadigm triangulation, no synthesis.

### S3–4 · Plakoyiannaki et al. (2019), *MOR* 15(2) — Rethinking Qualitative Scholarship in Emerging Markets *(from general knowledge)*
**Abstract.** Argues qualitative research in emerging markets too often imports
Western templates; calls for **context-sensitive** researching, theorizing, and
reporting that treats context as substantive, not backdrop.
**Questions:** Is my study importing a Western frame onto a local context? How do I
make the local context substantive, not scenery?

### S3–4 · Köhler et al. (2022), *ORM* 25(2) — Templates in Qualitative Research Methods *(from general knowledge)*
**Abstract.** Reviews the dominant templates (Gioia methodology, Eisenhardt cases,
grounded theory). Templates aid rigor & legitimacy **but** can ossify into
box-ticking and crowd out novelty; proposes using them reflexively.
**Questions:** Which template am I defaulting to, and is it appropriate — or am I
choosing it for reviewer safety? (ties to Bansal & Corley's "premature convergence")

### S3–4 · Tracy (2010), *Qual. Inquiry* 16(10): 837–851 — Eight "Big-Tent" Criteria *(verified from PDF)*
**Core argument — separate _ends_ from _means_.** Tracy offers **eight universal
end-goals** of high-quality qualitative research (the *what*), each reachable by a
*variety* of methods, crafts and practices (the *means* — the *how*). This lets
scholars **across paradigms** (positivist → interpretive → critical → poststructural)
share a common language of quality **without** agreeing on one method — a "big tent."
Analogy: cheesemakers all agree good cheese has "mouthfeel" (an end), though brie,
feta and cheddar reach it by different processes. Criteria are useful as a
**pedagogical** tool (help novices learn the craft) and a **political** one (communicate
value to power-holders/funders/IRBs who misjudge qualitative work) — while leaving room
for "dialogue, imagination, growth, improvisation." Her caveat: *"if it really were as
straightforward as eight simple criteria, there would be no magic, no surprises, no
genius."*

**The eight criteria (end-goal → key means, from Table 1):**
1. **Worthy topic** — relevant, timely, significant, interesting; often *counterintuitive*
   (aim for "that's interesting," not "that's obvious").
2. **Rich rigor** — sufficient, abundant, *appropriate & complex* use of theory, data,
   time in the field, samples, contexts, and analysis. "Requisite variety" — the sensing
   instrument must be as complex as the phenomenon. *(Necessary but not sufficient.)*
3. **Sincerity** — **self-reflexivity** about the researcher's values/biases + **transparency**
   about methods and challenges (an audit trail); *show* reflexivity, don't just tell it.
4. **Credibility** — **thick description** & concrete detail (show, don't tell), tacit
   knowledge; **triangulation/crystallization**; **multivocality**; **member reflections**
   (collaborative — richer than mere "member checks").
5. **Resonance** — the work *moves/affects* readers via aesthetic/evocative writing,
   **naturalistic generalization**, and **transferability** (readers map it to their own
   situation) — how qualitative work "generalizes" without statistics.
6. **Significant contribution** — conceptual/theoretical, practical, moral, methodological,
   or heuristic (extends knowledge, improves practice, or empowers).
7. **Ethics** — four layers: **procedural** (IRB, informed consent, do-no-harm),
   **situational** (context-specific), **relational** (ethic of care/mutual respect),
   and **exiting** (how you leave the scene & share results without harm).
8. **Meaningful coherence** — the study **achieves its stated purpose**, uses methods that
   *fit* its goals/paradigm, and meaningfully **interconnects literature ↔ RQs ↔ findings
   ↔ interpretation.**

**Why this is a high-leverage reading.** It's a ready-made **quality checklist** for
designing *and* defending a term-project study — and it cross-links the courses:
*sincerity/credibility/coherence* ↔ CRO (Pratt, Jonsen on persuasive qualitative writing);
the *four ethics* ↔ procedural/relational/situational/exiting layers of any ethics-review
package; *meaningful coherence* ↔ Dr. Sengupta's S1 principle that **RQ ↔ methodology ↔
analysis must stay internally consistent.**

**Questions / application:**
- **Rich rigor:** is my interview sample + time-in-field "sufficient, appropriate, complex"
  for the study, or am I under-powering it for convenience?
- **Sincerity:** what is my positionality, and which biases should I **show reflexively**
  rather than hide?
- **Credibility:** plan **member reflections** (share findings back with participants) +
  **triangulation** (multiple data sources).
- **Resonance vs. rigor:** is "resonance" (reader impact) a real quality marker or a
  rhetorical dodge — and which of the 8 is my study weakest on right now?
- **Coherence:** do my RQs, method, and analysis actually line up end-to-end?

### S5 · Lê & Schmid (2022), *ORM* 25(2) — The Practice of Innovating Research Methods *(from general knowledge)*
**Abstract.** Studies *how* scholars actually invent/adapt methods in practice —
innovation as situated craft, not one-off genius.
**Questions:** Where could my study justifiably innovate method, and how do I defend
that to reviewers?

### S5 · Bansal et al. (2018), *AMJ* 61(4) — New Ways of Seeing Through Qualitative Research *(from general knowledge)*
**Abstract.** Editors' call to widen qualitative repertoire — novel data, questions,
and forms of seeing. (Sibling to the 2011 Bansal & Corley pre-read.)
**Questions:** What can qualitative work "see" in my topic that survey/quant work can't?

## Module II — Qualitative Models, Data Sources & Collection

### S6–8 · Ivanova-Gongne et al. (2018), *Ind. Marketing Mgmt* 70 — Collecting Rich Qualitative Data (CEE) *(from general knowledge)*
**Abstract.** Practical challenges of interview/relationship data in
transition/emerging economies (access, trust, language, culture) + plausible
solutions.
**Questions:** What access/trust barriers will I face recruiting participants to speak
candidly? What's my consent + confidentiality plan?

### S9–12 · Eisenhardt & Graebner (2007), *AMJ* 50(1) — Theory Building From Cases
**Abstract.** How to build testable, generalizable theory from **multiple cases**;
theoretical (not random) sampling; replication logic; managing the tension between
rich story and positivist reviewer expectations.
**Questions:** Is my study better as multi-case or single-phenomenon interview study?
What's my sampling logic?

### S9–12 · Wiesche et al. (2017), *MISQ* 41(3) — Grounded Theory in IS Research *(from general knowledge)*
**Abstract.** How grounded theory is (and should be) applied in information-systems
research — coding, theoretical sampling, saturation, common misuses.
**Questions:** Am I doing *grounded theory* or just *thematic coding*? (they're not
the same — be precise)

### S9–12 · Cunliffe (2010), *ORM* 13(2) — Retelling Tales of the Field (Ethnography) *(from general knowledge)*
**Abstract.** Revisits Van Maanen; ethnography's craft and the researcher's
positionality/voice; different "problematics" (subjectivist assumptions) behind
qualitative work.
**Questions:** What's my positionality relative to the people I'm studying — asset or
bias?

### S9–12 · Gill (2014), *ORM* 17(2) — Phenomenology for Organizational Research *(from general knowledge)*
**Abstract.** Makes the case for phenomenology (lived experience) in org research;
distinguishes descriptive vs interpretive (hermeneutic) phenomenology.
**Questions:** Is "how people *experience* X" a phenomenological question — and would
that fit my RQ better than grounded theory?

## Module III — Analysis, Theorizing & Reporting

### S13–16 · Lester et al. (2020), *HRD Review* 19(1) — Learning to Do Qualitative Data Analysis *(from general knowledge)*
**Abstract.** A practical starting-point guide: data prep, coding, theme
development, moving from codes to interpretation — aimed at novices.
**Questions:** What's my concrete analysis pipeline (transcription → coding tool →
themes)? Manual, NVivo, or Atlas.ti?

### S13–16 · Thomann & Maggetti (2020), *Soc. Methods & Research* 49(2) — Designing Research with QCA *(from general knowledge)*
**Abstract.** Qualitative Comparative Analysis — set-theoretic, case-based method
for **configurations** of conditions leading to outcomes; approaches, challenges, tools.
**Questions:** Could QCA identify *combinations* of conditions that produce my outcome?
Do I have enough cases for it?

### S17 · Jonsen et al. (2018), *ORM* 21(1) — Convincing Qualitative Research: Persuasive Writing *(from general knowledge)*
**Abstract.** What makes qualitative write-ups *persuasive* — transparency,
evidence trail, rhetorical craft (echoes Bansal & Corley on craftsmanship).
**Questions:** How will I show the data→theory trail so a skeptical reviewer is
convinced, not just told?
